## Context

Can the day and place of a recorded Chicago incident predict its crime type? The task was to classify each incident in
the City of Chicago's public records for 2020 to 2022, using only when and where it was recorded.

<More label="The data and its class imbalance">
The records hold 656,549 incidents across 33 crime types, described by community area, type of location, day, month
and year. The classes are very uneven: theft, battery and criminal damage account for 51% of the records, the five
most common types for 68%, and seven types have fewer than 100 records each.

## Approach

The team compared seven classifiers (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, naive Bayes, k-nearest
neighbours, support vector machine and a neural network), each evaluated with the same stratified cross-validation.
Teammates cleaned and explored the data beforehand.

<Flow
  label="The modelling pipeline"
  steps={['Encode and scale features', 'Random 80/20 split', 'Stratified 3-fold cross-validation', 'Compare seven model families', 'Grid search for the neural network']}
/>

<More label="How the split, the folds and the preprocessing fit together">
- **Preprocessing:** the type of location was coded as integers and every feature was scaled to the range 0 to 1,
  across all records, before the split.
- **80/20 split:** a random split with a fixed seed; 80% of the records were kept for training and model selection.
- **3-fold cross-validation:** the folds are cut from the 80% training part. Each fold trains on two thirds of it and is
  scored on the remaining third.
- **Stratification:** the folds keep the share of each crime type similar. The rarest type has a single record, so it
  cannot appear in every fold.
</More>

<More label="The neural-network grid search">
The grid covers four layer shapes, two activation functions, two solvers, three regularisation strengths and two
learning-rate schedules, 96 configurations in all, each scored by the same stratified 3-fold cross-validation.
</More>

## Findings and limitations

Predicting crime type from date and place alone proved hard. Always predicting theft is right for about 21% of
incidents, and the saved cross-validation runs scored only modestly above that across 33 crime types. The saved
notebooks do not hold a final comparison of all seven families.

<More label="Limits of the evaluation and of the data">
- Accuracy rewards the common types. No per-class scores were saved, so the results cannot show how well rare types
  were recognised.
- The records are incidents reported to and recorded by the police, not all crime, and the features are limited to
  place, day, month and year. Time of day was not used.
- Scaling was fitted on all records before the split; fitting it on the training folds only would be stricter.
- The type of location was coded as plain integers, which imposes an order that the categories do not have.
- The 20% test part was not scored in the saved notebooks.
</More>
